Paper Constellations

You are reading one paper. Each map below puts it in the middle and answers one question about what surrounds it: where it came from, who made it, what it sits beside, what is inside it, and what you already own. Drag any map to turn it. Hover a point for its paper.

The example data is a stand-in built around Attention Is All You Need so the shapes are recognisable. None of these numbers are real.

01

Ancestry: what it stands on, what stands on it

Where did this paper come from, and where did it go?

drag · time runs upward
this paper background uses its method compares results thick = influential · size = citations

The shape

Time is the vertical axis. The paper floats at its own year. Everything it cites hangs below it, everything that cites it rises above. A faint second layer shows the references of the references and the citers of the citers, so the paper reads as a waist in an hourglass.

Encoding

  • Height: publication year, so an old reference sits far down and a preprint from last month sits just overhead.
  • Edge colour: Semantic Scholar's citation intent. A paper that cites this one for its method is a different kind of descendant from one that cites it in passing.
  • Edge weight: influential citations are drawn thick. The thick edges upward are the papers worth opening next.
  • Point size: citation count, log scale.

Interaction

  • Hover opens the citation card the app already has. Click opens it in Discover.
  • Click a node and it becomes the waist: the map re-centres and its own ancestry fills in.
  • A year slider at the side clips the top, so you can watch the descendants arrive.
Cost: two OpenAlex calls plus one Semantic Scholar call for intents, all of which the app already makes for other reasons. 3D: earns its keep; the depth axis separates the two hundred citers so they do not pile onto one line.
02

Author orbits: who they work with

Who are these authors, and who do they keep coming back to?

drag · nearer orbit = more papers together
this paper its authors co-authors works with two of them their papers

The shape

The paper is the centre. Its authors are suns placed around it. Each sun carries its own system: the people this author has published with most, orbiting at a distance set by how many papers they share. A co-author who works with two of the suns sits in a shared orbit between them, and that is where the lab, or the standing collaboration, becomes visible.

Encoding

  • Orbit radius: inverse of papers co-written. The inner ring is the people they always write with.
  • Sun size: h-index from the author card the app already fetches.
  • Small grey satellites: the papers themselves, parked on the orbit of the collaboration that produced them.
  • Ring colour: the author's main OpenAlex topic, so a sun whose rings are all one colour is a specialist.

Interaction

  • Hover a sun opens the author card. Hover a satellite opens the paper.
  • A time toggle turns each orbit into a spiral, year by year outward, so you can see a collaboration that ended.
  • Pin a co-author to see every paper they share with the suns listed on the right.
Cost: one OpenAlex works query per author, which the author card half does already. 3D: worthwhile when a paper has eight authors; at two authors a flat pair of rings is enough.
03

Neighbourhood: the papers it is most relevant to

What is this paper in conversation with, whether or not they cite each other?

drag · nearer = more related
this paper cited together share references topics

The shape

A cloud, not a tree. Relatedness is distance from the centre, computed three ways and blended: papers often cited alongside this one (co-citation), papers that cite the same things (bibliographic coupling), and OpenAlex's own related_works. The result includes papers with no citation edge to this one at all, which is the point: the rival method from another lab is here, two hops away in citation terms but one hop away in meaning.

Encoding

  • Distance: blended relatedness score, nearest at the centre.
  • Colour: OpenAlex primary topic, so clusters of the same hue are subfields.
  • Solid link: cited together at least five times. Dashed: share a quarter of their references.
  • A paper you have already read is drawn filled, one you have not is hollow, so the unread part of the neighbourhood stands out.

Interaction

  • Hover for the card. Drag a cluster out to see it as its own neighbourhood.
  • A list beside the map names the twenty nearest, with + to save each to a collection, the way Discover does.
Cost: coupling needs the reference lists of each citer, so about fifty calls, cached per paper. 3D: moderate; a cloud is genuinely volumetric, but a 2D force layout tells nearly the same story.
04

Field terrain: where it sits in the landscape

Is this paper on a well-trodden peak, or out on its own?

drag · height = citations
this paper same first author papers in the field ridge = heavily cited ground

The shape

A map in the geographic sense. The ground plane is a two-dimensional projection of paper embeddings (Semantic Scholar's SPECTER vectors, or OpenAlex topic scores where there is no vector). Height is how heavily cited the ground is around each point. Peaks are the crowded, settled parts of the field. This paper is a flag planted somewhere on it, and the first author's other papers are pins, so you also see their path across the terrain over the years.

Encoding

  • Position: UMAP of embeddings for the few hundred papers nearest this one, computed once in a worker.
  • Height: kernel-smoothed citation count, log scale.
  • Pins joined in year order make the author's trail. A long jump is a change of field.

Interaction

  • Hover a pin for the paper. Hover the ground for the topic name the nearest papers share.
  • Click a peak to list the papers that make it, most cited first.
Cost: the embeddings are one batched Semantic Scholar call; UMAP in the browser for 500 points is a second or two. 3D: the whole idea is the third axis, so yes. This is the most striking of the six and the hardest to make trustworthy, since the projection moves a little every time it is recomputed.
05

Inside the paper: a citation mind map

Which references does each part of the paper lean on?

drag · spokes are sections
this paper sections references · size = times cited cited in two sections

The shape

This is the one that is literally a mind map. The paper is the hub, its sections are the spokes, and every entry in the bibliography hangs off the section that cites it. The app already finds each [12] on the page and knows the bibliography entry it names, so the only new work is reading which section heading the mark sits under. A reference cited in both the introduction and the results gets a cord between the two spokes, and those cords are the paper's argument: the thing it set up and then measured against.

Encoding

  • Spoke length: how many citations the section makes. A long related-work spoke and a short methods spoke says the contribution is small and the survey is thorough.
  • Leaf size: times cited within the paper.
  • Leaf colour: whether you have it in the library, so the unread references of the methods section are obvious.

Interaction

  • Click a leaf and the page scrolls to the first place it is cited, with the citation flashed, the way In the bibliography does now.
  • Click a spoke to jump to the section.
  • Your highlights appear as small marks on the spokes at the place in the section they were made.
Cost: no network at all; everything is on the page. 3D: not really needed. A radial 2D map is clearer. Shown in 3D here only so it can be compared with the others on equal terms.
06

Your shelf: the map with your library laid over it

Of everything near this paper, what have I read, and what should I read next?

drag · ghosts are not in your library
finished reading not started not in library read next

The shape

The same neighbourhood as map 3, but the ink is yours. Finished papers are solid, half-read ones are half-filled, saved but untouched ones are hollow, and papers you do not own are dashed ghosts. Each collection is a translucent halo around its members. Three ghosts glow: the ones with the most edges into the solid part of the map, which is a defensible definition of read next.

Encoding

  • Fill: reading state from the library, the same three states Home uses.
  • Halo colour: collection. A paper in two collections sits where two halos overlap.
  • Glow: the read-next score, which is edges into finished papers minus edges into ghosts.

Interaction

  • + on a ghost saves it to the collection whose halo it is nearest.
  • The Today view on Home could use the same score for start the next paper instead of picking by date added.
  • Hovering a solid node shows your highlights from it, so the map doubles as a memory of what you took from each.
Cost: none beyond map 3; the library is local. 3D: same as map 3. The value here is the overlay, not the dimension.

Where it lives in Reader

A third tab in the dock, beside Discover and Highlights, called Map, on M. The dock is narrow, so the map is a small turning globe there with a switch for which map, and ⇧M or the expand corner opens it as a floating window the way Ask AI does, over the paper.

Every point reuses what already exists: hovering shows the citation or author card, clicking opens the paper in Discover with Save, Read and Source, and + saves to the collection chosen on Home. The map is a different way of looking at the same records, so nothing new is stored, and the one thing to cache is the neighbourhood per paper, in the same place the citation lookups are kept for the session.

On Home, the Find papers suggestions row (near the paper you are reading) is already the nearest ten of map 3. The row could turn into the map when the dock is away.
